How to read vote attendance Full definition → Glossary

88%: out of 100 recorded votes held since their first vote of the legislature, the person voted in 88 — yea, nay or abstention, whichever way.

  • The count starts at each person's first vote: someone elected mid-term is not counted absent from votes held before they arrived.
  • —: the Assembly's President* and its three Vice-Presidents. To stay neutral, they vote little or not at all: a low rate would not mean they were absent.
  • n/a: no recorded vote for this person.
  • This is not an official measure: a missed vote does not tell why.

* The Assembly's President is not the same as the Premier: the President runs the debates and stays neutral, while the Premier heads the government and votes like the other members. See the Assembly's presidency in the Glossary →

** Updated every morning: the site rereads the Assembly's register of votes and recalculates each person's rate, then the median, the colours and the numbers above. A vote held one day shows up the next; during the dissolution, nothing changes.

✋ What is “challenging”?

One click on “Ask for an explanation” = a citizen request for the bill's sponsor to explain it in plain language, and for us to keep an eye on it together.

An account (email) is required — one request per person, no anonymous requests. 🔥 At 1,000, we push for an official petition.

How to read a vote

Recorded division

Each MLA's name is recorded in the Votes and Proceedings, the Assembly's official minutes. It's the only kind of vote that can be attributed to a person.

Passed ≠ law

A winning vote moves the bill one stage forward. It only becomes law after assent by the Lieutenant-Governor.

Party line

Most votes follow the party's instruction. Free votes are rare — and that's where the nominal detail gets interesting.

Toutes les promesses

Where these promises come from

  • From the parties themselves. Every quote is taken from a document the party published — an electoral platform or a campaign announcement — and reproduced word for word. A quote that cannot be found verbatim in the source is rejected automatically. The “See the source” link on each card leads to the original.
  • These are not the “flagship” promises. At most 8 per party, selected automatically from the most concrete ones — amounts, targets, deadlines. Nothing here ranks commitments by importance. A central commitment stated without a figure may therefore be missing, and the parties have published more.
  • The period covered is not the same for everyone. Québec solidaire, the Liberal Party and the Conservative Party publish a full platform, read in its entirety. The Parti Québécois does not: its commitments are read from its campaign announcements since the August 27 dissolution.
  • The Coalition avenir Québec is absent. It has published commitments on its website, but that site asks automated tools not to read it. We do not work around that request. It will be added as soon as a readable source exists.
  • Nothing is taken from the news media. A commitment announced at a press conference but never published by the party does not appear here. Quebec news organizations have opted their content out of AI use, and our permission requests remain unanswered.

In short: an absence from this list says nothing about what a party thinks. For the full list, follow the link to the source.

The lobbyist registry

Who's paid to influence public decisions — and how to look it up

Anyone paid to communicate with public office holders (ministers, MNAs, senior officials…) about laws, regulations, grants or contracts must register in a public registry, overseen by the Quebec Lobbying Commissioner, an independent institution. Lobbyists must declare their mandates there.

Why this site doesn't reproduce the registry: its data isn't offered as open data (nothing on Données Québec) and is only accessible through the official search tool. Rather than make a copy that wouldn't be reliable or up to date, we link straight to the official public tools.

Parliamentary glossary

To understand the rest of the site without a political science degree
Bill
Process
The text a member or the government submits to the Assembly to become a law. It only becomes a real law once passed and then assented to.
Passage in principle
Process
The first big stage: the Assembly votes on whether it agrees with the general idea of the bill, before studying it in clause-by-clause detail.
Clause-by-clause review
Process
The stage where a committee (a small group of MLAs) goes through the bill in detail, clause by clause, and can propose amendments.
Passage
Process
The final vote in the Chamber on the entire bill, once all previous stages are complete.
Assent
Process
The very last administrative step: the Lieutenant Governor gives official assent, and the bill formally becomes a law of Saskatchewan.
Parliamentary committee
Process
A smaller group of MLAs who study a topic or bill in depth, hear experts and citizens, before it comes back before the full Assembly.
Recorded vote
Votes
A vote where each MLA's name and choice (Yea/Nay) are officially recorded — as opposed to a voice vote, where only the overall result is noted.
Minutes (Procès-verbal)
Votes
The official but summarized record of everything that happened during a sitting: decisions, votes, tabled documents.
Debates journal (Journal des débats)
Votes
The complete, word-for-word transcript of everything said in the Chamber — unlike the minutes, which only summarize decisions.
House leader
People & roles
The MLA responsible for organizing their group's proceedings at the Assembly — a bit like the conductor of the calendar and debates for their party.
Whip
People & roles
The MLA in charge of making sure members of their caucus are present for votes and follow the party line.
Minister Delegate / Minister Responsible for
People & roles
A minister who isn't the main head of a department, but who officially holds responsibility for a specific file within or alongside it.
Attendance (%)
Citizen tools
The figure shown under each minister (e.g. “attendance: 88% (647/735)”): out of the 735 recorded votes across the legislature's 3 sessions, 647 are ones where this person appears as Yea, Nay, or Abstention — meaning they were present to vote, regardless of which way. The count starts from their very first detected vote (a way to approximate the start of their current term without external data). The Assembly does not publish official attendance; this is a presence proxy, not an official measure — the same method used by some media outlets (e.g. Radio-Canada).

Where this data comes from, and how to go further

Public sources used here

Bills — Données Québec, the National Assembly's official dataset, updated in real time.
Bill details (sponsor, current stage, full text as PDF) — assnat.qc.ca, each bill's individual page.
Bill summaries — generated by artificial intelligence (Claude API) from the official full text as introduced; every summary is labelled as such on the site.
Vote registry — assnat.qc.ca, the detail page of every recorded vote across the 3 sessions of the 43rd Legislature, including each MNA's name and party.
Cabinet — quebec.ca, the official Council of Ministers page, scraped live.
MNAs and official emails — assnat.qc.ca, the official list of sitting MNAs.
Petitions — assnat.qc.ca, the official list of open petitions.
Attendance (%) — computed from the vote registry above (see the glossary definition); no external source.

Hi! My name is Martin Archambault I'm 45, and dossiersaskatchewan.ca is your first step into democracy!

If we want people to understand politics, it has to be brought down to their level.

When I first tried to take an interest in it, on top of not knowing the jargon, bills came as two lines… and fifty PDFs. Most people would simply walk away.

So I had an idea: use artificial intelligence first to read through all of it, then to put it in plain words. That's how DossierQuébec was born.

Alongside the AI recap, I've given people a lexicon that explains the basics of political jargon. Plain-language law exists — think of this as its equivalent for politics!
